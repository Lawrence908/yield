The Inverted Yield Curve

When the 3-month Treasury out-yields the 10-year, the curve is inverted, and since the late 1960s a US recession has followed within about two years nearly every time. This page keeps score: the spread daily since 1982 and monthly back to 1953, every inversion episode measured against what actually followed, every recession shaded. Nothing here is a forecast; the one probability shown is the New York Fed's, as published.

The spreads, daily

Percentage points: 10-year minus 3-month Treasury constant-maturity yields, as published by the Federal Reserve. Below the zero line, the curve is inverted. Shaded bands are NBER recessions, peak month through trough. Both published spreads are drawn: 10y−2y is the one headlines quote, 10y−3m the one research and the New York Fed prefer and the one the rest of this page is built on. They are different measures rather than a measure and a variant, and they invert at different times, so the gap between them is worth seeing rather than being told about. They start on different dates because the published series do: 10y−2y from June 1976, 10y−3m from January 1982, the latter bounded by when the 3-month constant maturity begins. Either can be switched off.

Un-inversion is not an all-clear; in this record it is often the last act before the downturn. The start and end columns above let you check that claim against every row.

The legs: which end moved

The chart above gives the 10y−2y spread as one line against zero. This is the same thing taken apart: the two yields themselves, so you can see which leg did the moving. Normally the curve slopes up and the 10-year pays more, because money locked away longer carries more risk and wants compensating. An inverted curve is that ordering reversed, short-term debt yielding more than long-term debt of the same credit quality, which is the market pricing in lower rates later. Inversion is only ever defined against a stated pair; this is the 10-year against the 2-year, the pair the headlines mean.

The band between the lines is that spread, shaded grey while the curve is the right way up and red wherever the 2-year is paying more than the 10-year, so the inversion is drawn rather than inferred from two lines crossing. The strip under the axis marks the same inverted stretches at a constant height, because at wider ranges a half-point inversion is only a few pixels of shading: it answers when, not how long, and the briefest crossings are drawn a touch wider than they lasted. The 2-year begins in June 1976 and the 10-year in 1962, so Max starts where both are quoted. Reading it: 2022 was the short end rocketing while the long end sat; the late-1970s episodes were both ends chasing inflation upward together. All tenors adds the 3-month, 1, 3, 5 and 30-year as thin lines, shaded short to long, for when the question is where the rest of the curve sat rather than what these two did.

The Bank of Canada publishes the legs, not the spread, so the subtraction happens here: 10-year benchmark minus 3-month bill, daily from 2001 because that is the Valet platform's depth, not the bond market's. The dotted monthly series reaches back to 1956 via the OECD, whose short leg is an interbank rate rather than a bill; where the two overlap the gap between them is that basis difference, shown rather than smoothed over. The New York Fed's probability

The one forecast on this page, and it is not ours.

The New York Fed reads this same monthly spread through a probit model and publishes the probability of a US recession within twelve months. Their latest: … by …, estimated from … data. Details and history at their FAQ.

They publish it as a legacy spreadsheet, so the value is entered here by hand and dated: last checked …. A number that looks fresh because a script touched a file would be worse than one that is honestly dated. This page computes no probabilities of its own.

Sources and construction

The core series is published, not computed here:

spread (pp) = 10-year constant-maturity yield − 3-month constant-maturity yield

Where this page does compute (the monthly and pre-1982 spreads), the 3-month bill's discount-basis rate is first converted to a bond-equivalent yield, the New York Fed's construction, with d the discount rate as a decimal:

BEY = 365 d / (360 − 91 d)

United States

  • Daily spreads, as published: FRED T10Y3M (from 1982-01-04) and T10Y2Y (from 1976-06-01), both from Federal Reserve H.15 constant-maturity yields. Legs: DGS10 (daily from 1962) and DGS3MO (daily from September 1981).
  • Monthly to 1953: GS10 minus BEY(TB3MS), computed here, both raw legs shipped alongside so the arithmetic is checkable. The construction was verified against the spread column of the NY Fed's own workbook across their 1959-onward sample.
  • Daily 1962-1981 segment: DGS10 minus BEY(DTB3), the secondary-market bill rate, a different basis from the constant-maturity spread. Its own labelled series; nothing on this page is spliced.
  • The 2-year leg: DGS2, Federal Reserve H.15 constant maturity, daily from 1976-06-01, on the same bond-equivalent basis as the legs above. It is the short line on the legs chart and it gives T10Y2Y its own checkable legs, which the published spread otherwise would not have here.
  • The other tenors, drawn only behind that chart's All tenors toggle: DGS30 (daily from 1977-02-15), plus DGS5, DGS3 and DGS1 (all daily from 1962-01-02), same H.15 constant-maturity basis. One caveat: Treasury suspended the 30-year bond between February 2002 and February 2006, and the values FRED carries across that window come from the Treasury's long-term extrapolation factor rather than an auctioned 30-year, so that stretch of the 30-year line is a different construction from the rest of it.
  • Recession bands: NBER dates via USREC, … recessions since 1854 in the shared dataset, drawn peak month through trough.

Canada

  • Daily from 2001: Bank of Canada Valet, keyless, series BD.CDN.10YR.DQ.YLD minus TB.CDN.90D.MID.
  • Monthly from 1956: OECD Main Economic Indicators via FRED, IRLTLT01CAM156N minus IR3TIB01CAM156N. The short leg is an interbank rate, not a bill; stated wherever it is drawn.
  • Recession bands: C.D. Howe Institute Business Cycle Council chronology (Commentary 366, Table 1), COVID-19 dated by the Council's 2021 declaration. No API exists; the dates are curated with citations.

Plumbing

  • FRED is fetched keyless-first (the public CSV endpoint), keyed API as fallback; Valet has no keys at all. If FRED went dark entirely, the US daily legs would rebuild from the Treasury's own par yield curve XML (1990 onward) and the page would say so.
  • Refreshes twice each weekday via cron, after the H.15 afternoon posting; the stamp above says when data last moved. Yields are near-final at publication, so most runs verify rather than change.
  • Series follow the shared econ-core contract (id, provenance, units, frequency, confidence, observations, splices) used across jobs, debt and diesel, with a vintages field reserved for as-published (ALFRED) views: designed in now, populated later.
  • Raw data: /api/data, or the files under /data/.
